Godel, Cohen and the continuum hypothesis ## The continuum hypothesis - In 1874, Cantor built a $1:1$ map between the *naturals* and the *algebraic numbers*. - By 1878, Cantor had given two proofs that the cardinality of the *reals* was greater than that of the *naturals*, his *diagonalisation argument*. - Question: is there a set with *more* elements than the *naturals* and *fewer* elements than the reals? - In symbols $\exists S \mid \aleph_0 < \vert S \vert < 2^{\aleph_0}$ - The continuum hypothesis conjectures that this statement is false, i.e., *there is no set with more elements than the integers and fewer elements than the naturals.*
algebraic numbers: numbers given by solutions to polynomial equations (e.g., 9x^4 - x^3 + 5x^2 - 2) $2^\aleph_0$ is the cardinality of the real numbers. recall $2^X$ is the powerset of $x$ (all possible subsets of $x$). Cantor showed that the real numbers the same cardinality as the powerset o the naturals.
## Independent of ZFC - For rigorous proof, mathematicians must agree on a *set of axioms*. - Accepted axioms for set theory are called *Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms (ZF)* - Godel (of Incompleteness Theorems fame) showed in 1938 that, in ZF set theory, the continuum hypothesis cannot be *disproved*, I.e., the continuum hypothesis is *consistent* with the axioms of ZF set theory. - Later, Cohen (Fields Medal, 1966) showed that in ZF set theory, the continuum hypothesis cannot be *proved*. I.e., the continuum hypothesis is *not inconsistent* with the axioms of ZF set theory.
examples of ZF axioms: axiom of the empty set, i.e., there exists a set containing no elements, axiom of the power set: every set has a power set
## The parallel postulate
- Euclid gave four basic axioms of geometry. - 5th axiom: *parallel postulate* - cannot be disproved in plane geometry, - but cannot be proved in spherical geometry. - Consistent with the axioms of both. ![](./pp.png){width=90%}
Parallel postulate: if you have two lines extending from a third line, and the sum of the interior angles is less that 180, then the two lines will eventually cross. can be proved from axioms in plane geometry but we can construct a counterexample in spherical geometry. i.e., there is a universe where it's true and a universe where it's false
## Independence of ZFC - Can we construct a *model* of ZFC (universe) in which the continuum hypothesis is true? - Hard, actually impossible, due to Godel's incompleteness Theorems. - Let's assume we have a model $M$; Godel constructed a model $L$ in which the CH is true. - Then Cohen used the same starting model, $M$, to construct a new model $N$ where the continuum hypothesis is false. ## Godel - Assume we have a model $M$ of ZFC - Constructed a subset $L \subset M$ where the continuum hypothesis is true - Called *Godel constructible universe*. ## Cohen - Assume we have (the same model as Godel used) $M$ of ZFC - Extended the model to construct a superset $N \supset M$ where the CH is false. - I.e., we have a fixed model of ZFC, $M$ and two new models $L \subset M \subset N$ where - CH is *true* in $L$, and - CH is *false* in $N$. ## Philosophies - *Pluralists*: we're done, either decide whether to make CH an axiom (CH is true), or *not CH* nd axiom (CH is false) (e.g., Cohen) - *Non-pluralists*: we need to find more axioms which are sufficient to either prove or disprove CH (e.g., Godel) ## Godel's program - Godel developed *large cardinal axioms* in an attempt to resolve CH. - Turned out to be very useful in resolving other previously independent problems, - but they were not sufficient to resolve CH. - Levy and Solovay and others proved that large cardinal axioms are sufficient to resolve problems which are strictly simpler than CH, but not sufficient to solve CH. # Combinations, permutations, and goats ## Objectives - ## Probability - Measurement of the likelihood of an event. - Can be used to analyse historical data or to make future predictions - Probability has many and varied applications. ## Probability $$ \text{Probability of event }e = \dfrac{\text{number of occurances of event }e}{\text{total number of events}} $$ ## Dice
- Probability of rolling a particular number of an $n$-sided fair die is $1/n$. - E.g., for a $6$-sided fair die, the probability of rolling a $4$ (or any other number) is $1/6$. - Physical dice are platonic solids $4$, $6$, $8$, $12$ or $20$ sides. - Computer programs can model any number of sides of course, but there true randomness is a problem. ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_0.png){width=80%}

## Observer problems are a serious matter - In non-Newtonian physics (Einsteinian Relativity) - The world exists relative to the observer (time dialation and what have you). - Things become more complicated when considering quantum theory. ## Lets talk cards - Standard deck of $52$ playing cards. - Four suits: $\heartsuit, \spadesuit, \diamondsuit, \clubsuit$. - Thirteen cards of each suit $A, 2, 3, \ldots, 9, 10, J, Q, K$. ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_22.png){width=60%} ## Drawing cards - From a newly shuffled deck of playing cards, what is the probability of drawing - A particular card (e.g., $K \heartsuit$)? - A particular suit (e.g., $\spadesuit$)? - A particular rank (e.g., $8$)? - A picture card $A, J, Q, K$ of any suit? - A particular colour (red or black)?
A particular card (e.g., $K \heartsuit$)? $1/52$ A particular suit (e.g., $\spadesuit$)? $1/4$ A particular rank (e.g., $8$)? $4/52 = 1/13$ A picture card $A, J, Q, K$ of any suit? $4/13$ A particular colour (red or black)? $1/2$
## Drawn and not replaced - Once a card is drawn (and not replaced), it is removed from the possible options. - This alters probabilities, e.g., - First card drawn is $Q \heartsuit$ -- $1/52$. - What is the probability of choosing the $K \clubsuit$ next? - $1/51$, since there are $51$ cards remaining. ## A combination of $5$ cards $$1/52 \cdot 1/51 \cdot 1/50 \cdot 1/49 \cdot 1/48$$ - or $1/311,875,200$ - $\approx 0.0000000032 = 3,2 \cdot 10^-9$ - Note: with multiple players, you need to take their draws into account, too. ## The Binomial Coefficient - Suppose we have a set of $n$ possibilities, - We want to select $k$ of them, - We write ${n \choose k} = \dfrac{n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots (n - k + 1)}{k \cdot (k-1) \cdots 2 \cdot 1}$. - If we draw $5$ cards from a deck of $52$, we write ${52 \choose 5} = \dfrac{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48}{5 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1}$ - Read $n$ choose $k$, it means "from a set of $n$ elements, how many ways are there to choose $k$ of them?" ## How does this work? - We start out with a deck of $52$ cards. - We need to pick $5$ cards (ordered) - We take the first card -- now the deck contains $51$ cards, your hand contains $1$ card and you have $4$ spaces to fill. - You take a second card -- now the deck contains $51$ cards, your hand contains $2$ and you have $3$ spaces left to fill. - You keep taking cards... - Your fifth and final card is drawn from a deck of $52 - 4 = 49$ cards, leaving a deck of $48$ cards. ## The formula $$ {n\choose k} = \dfrac{n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots (n-k + 1)}{k \cdot (k-1) \cdot (k-2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1} $$ - Note that $k \cdot (k-1) \cdot (k-2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1 = k!$ (read $k$ factorial) - In fact, we can use factorials to simplify the formula further - $n! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots (n-k+1) \cdot (n-k) \cdot ((n-k)-1) \cdot ((n-k) - 2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$, - We can cancel out the $((n-k) - 1) \cdot ((n-k) - 2) \cdots 2 \cdot 1$ by dividing by $(n-k)!$ - So we get $n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdots (n-k+1) = \dfrac{n!}{n-k!}$. - Thus the formula becomes ${n \choose k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$ ## How many 5 card hands? $${n \choose k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$ ## Royal Flush A royal flush is $A, K, Q, J, 10$ of the same suit.
![](img/FoC-07a-prob_25.png){width=50%} ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_26.png){width=50%} ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_27.png){width=50%} ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_28.png){width=50%}
Every deck of cards contains $4$ royal flushes. ## Calculation - We have $4$ possible royal flushes, we need to choose $1$: ${4 \choose 1} = \dfrac{4}{1} = 4$ - ${52 \choose 5}$ or $2,598,960$ possible $5$-card hands - ...and $4$ of them are royal flushes. - Probability of getting a royal flush is $\dfrac{4}{2,598,960}$. - or $1$ in $649,740$, - or $0.000154\%$ ## What about 4 of a kind? - Four cards with the same rank, e.g., $2\heartsuit, 2\clubsuit, 2\diamondsuit, 2\spadesuit$ - For a given rank, we need to pick *all four* of its cards: $4 \choose 4$ - There are $13$ possible ranks, and our hand needs to contain four cards of that rank: $13 \choose 1$ - The fifth card must be of a different rank to all the others (one of the remaining $12$ possible ranks): $12 \choose 1$... - ...and it should belong to one of the four possible suits: $4 \choose 1$. ## What about 4 of a kind?
- Possible hands: ${13 \choose 1} \cdot {4 \choose 4} \cdot {12 \choose 1} \cdot {4 \choose 1}$ - $= 13 \cdot 1 \cdot 12 \cdot 4 = 624$ ![](img/FoC-07a-prob_29.png){width=70%} - $624/2,598,960$, - or $1/4165$, or - or $0.024\%$.
